Project at a glance

CasePilot AI turns a court order into a reviewable action plan (36 seconds in our first live test), and nothing enters the case until a lawyer approves it. It is an AI-powered case management app for Indian advocates, law firms and corporate legal teams.

Tagline: Less paperwork. Smarter case management. Never miss a critical task.

Live appcasepilot-neural-ninjas.web.app
Built withReact, Firebase (Mumbai region), Cloud Functions, Claude by Anthropic
Form factorWeb app that installs on Android, iOS and desktop as a progressive web app (PWA)

A lawyer uploads an order as a PDF or Word file. CasePilot reads it and proposes what needs to happen next: a summary, the court's directions, every date and a list of suggested tasks. Each finding cites the page it came from and a short quote. The server then checks that the quote really appears on that page.

The lawyer reviews each item side by side with the original document and approves, edits or rejects it. Approved items become tasks and timeline events that the team can assign, track and complete. Every action is recorded in an audit trail.

Our core idea is simple: every document should become an actionable, verifiable workflow. Most legal AI tools stop at a summary or a chatbot answer. CasePilot connects document intelligence directly to case management, with three commitments built in:

  • Source-linked. Every direction, date and fact points back to its page and quote.
  • Human-approved. AI output is only ever a proposal. It never becomes a task or a hearing date on its own.
  • Honest about uncertainty. A phrase like "within 14 days" is never silently turned into a confirmed deadline.

The problem

Legal work in India runs on documents that tell people what to do next, but the instructions are buried in dense text and scattered across tools. A missed direction or a misread date can cost a client their case.

Where the work lives today

A typical advocate's matter is spread across:

  • Court orders and judgments as PDFs, often downloaded from a court website or received over WhatsApp.
  • A diary or register of hearing dates, kept on paper or in Excel.
  • WhatsApp groups and email threads where juniors, clerks and clients ask "what's the next date?" and "what do we need to file?"
  • Personal memory, which is the real system of record in many practices.

None of these tools talk to each other. The order says what must happen; the diary says when; the team chat says who. Someone has to read every order, pull out each direction, work out the dates, and copy them into the other places by hand.

Why it goes wrong

A single interim order can carry several directions, each with its own responsible party and timeline. Consider this short, fictional example:

  1. The plaintiff shall file its updated statement of account within 14 days.
  2. The defendant may respond within 14 days after service of that statement.
  3. The matter is listed on 15 December 2026.
  4. Authorised representatives shall be present on the next date.

Four lines produce three different kinds of obligation. One deadline is relative to the order date. One is conditional on an event that has not happened yet. One is a fixed hearing date. A busy lawyer handling dozens of matters can easily miss that the second deadline cannot be calculated until service is recorded, or treat the hearing date as the filing deadline.

The risks multiply in firms and corporate legal teams. Work passes between partners, associates, paralegals and external counsel. Without one shared record, nobody can say with confidence which directions are complete, who owns each task, or whether a date was ever checked against the order.

Why existing tools fall short

  • Generic case diaries store dates someone typed in. They do not read the order, so the typing (and the errors) remain.
  • Generic AI chatbots can summarise an order, but the summary is a dead end. It does not become tasks or calendar entries, and it rarely shows exactly where each claim came from.
  • Unchecked AI output is dangerous in law. A model that invents a deadline, or quietly converts "within 14 days of service" into a fixed date, creates exactly the kind of error lawyers fear most.

Lawyers do not need another place to store files. They need to know what each document means for their next action, and they need to be able to trust that answer.

Objectives

Our goal is to cut the time from "order received" to "team knows what to do" from hours of manual reading to minutes of review, without ever taking the lawyer out of the decision.

Product objectives

  1. Turn documents into action. Every uploaded order should produce a structured summary, its directions, its dates and proposed tasks, ready for review.
  2. Make every AI claim verifiable. Each extracted item must cite a page and a quote that the server has checked against the document text. A citation that cannot be checked is flagged, never hidden.
  3. Keep the lawyer in control. No task, deadline or hearing date enters the case until an authorised person approves it. AI suggestions and approved work live in separate places.
  4. Handle uncertainty honestly. Relative deadlines stay unconfirmed until a lawyer enters a date. Conditional directions stay conditional. Unclear or missing text produces a warning.
  5. Give the team one shared record. Cases, documents, tasks, hearings and activity history in one workspace, with roles that match how Indian practices actually work.
  6. Protect confidential data. Separate workspaces per organisation, private storage, server-side permission checks and an audit trail from day one.

Hackathon definition of done

We set a concrete bar for the MVP before writing code:

A user can create a case, upload a sample court order, view an AI-generated summary with source references, review and approve suggested tasks, and track them from the case timeline. A second organisation cannot access any of those records.

What we deliberately do not do

Some features would make a flashier demo but would undermine trust. We ruled them out on purpose:

  • Predicting court outcomes.
  • Giving legal advice or legal strategy.
  • Filing documents or sending legal notices automatically.
  • Confirming an inferred deadline without a lawyer.
  • Building a full Indian case-law research engine.

CasePilot is a case coordinator, not a replacement for a lawyer's judgement.

How it works

The core workflow takes a court order from upload to tracked tasks in six steps, and the lawyer's approval sits between the AI and the case.

  1. Upload. A lawyer or paralegal drops a PDF or Word file (up to 15 MB) onto a case. The file goes straight to private storage with a live progress bar.
  2. Validate. The server checks the file's real signature, not just its name or type, and rejects encrypted PDFs, oversized archives and anything that is not a genuine PDF or DOCX. It fingerprints the file (SHA-256) and warns if the same document was already uploaded to the case.
  3. Extract text. A background job pulls out the text page by page for PDFs and section by section for Word files, so every later citation can point to an exact page. Blank pages or pages with no readable text produce a warning instead of a silent gap.
  4. Analyse with Claude. A second background job sends the page-marked text to Claude with a strict output schema. Claude returns a summary, the case details, facts, the court's directions, every date with its type, and proposed tasks. Each item carries a page number and a short supporting quote. Up to the first 60 pages are analysed, and the app says so if a document is longer.
  5. Verify citations. Before anyone sees the result, the server searches the actual page text for every quote. A quote found on the cited page is marked verified. A quote found on a different page is verified with the page corrected. A quote that cannot be found is flagged for the reviewer.
  6. Review and approve. The lawyer sees the original PDF beside the findings. Clicking a source jumps to its page. Each item can be approved, edited or rejected, and items with no warnings can be approved in bulk. Only approved items become tasks or timeline events.

From there, the work is ordinary case management, but grounded in the order:

  • Approved tasks appear on the case task board and in each person's cross-case task list, with owners, priorities, due dates and overdue flags.
  • Approved hearing dates appear on the case timeline and the firm calendar. The case's next hearing date updates automatically from verified hearings.
  • The dashboard shows upcoming hearings, urgent tasks, analyses waiting for review and recent activity.
  • Every approval, edit, rejection and completion lands in the audit trail.

The whole document pipeline runs in the background. The lawyer can close the tab after uploading; the status chips update live when they come back.

Responsible AI by design

We treat the AI as a fast, careful junior whose every claim must be checked, never as the decision-maker. Seven guardrails sit around the model, and most of them are enforced in code rather than left to the prompt.

GuardrailHow it worksWhy it matters
Structured outputClaude must return an object that matches a strict schema; the server validates it again before savingMalformed or partial answers never reach a lawyer
Citation checkThe server searches the real page text for each quote; found on the cited page is verified, found elsewhere is verified with the page corrected, not found is flaggedA lawyer can trust a green citation, and sees clearly which ones to check
Human approvalAI output is saved as a proposal; only an authorised reviewer's decision creates a task or eventThe AI cannot change a case on its own
No invented deadlinesRelative periods such as "within 14 days" keep their basis but get no date; the lawyer enters itThe most dangerous legal-AI error is designed out
Conditional directionsA direction that depends on an event (for example, service) is marked conditional, with its condition statedConditional work is not mistaken for a fixed obligation
Untrusted inputThe prompt tells the model to treat the document as data, and the server strips anything in the text that imitates a page markerA document cannot redirect the AI or forge a citation to another page
Visible uncertaintyUnclear text, blank pages and pages not analysed produce warnings shown on the review screenGaps are surfaced, not filled with guesses

The citation check in detail

Models sometimes quote text that is close to, but not exactly, what the document says, or cite the wrong page. Our verifier normalises both sides (case, punctuation, curly quotes, line breaks) and then looks for the quote on the cited page. Quotes shorter than eight characters are never treated as proof, because they match too easily. The result is stored with each item, so the review screen can mark it verified, page-corrected or unverified.

Approval is idempotent

Review decisions are saved in a single transaction. Each approved item creates its task or event with an ID derived from the analysis and the item, so a double-click, a network retry or a replayed request can never create a duplicate task. Each decision also writes its own audit entry.

Measuring the model, not just trusting it

We keep a live evaluation suite of fictional orders that calls Claude and checks its raw output before our server guardrails are applied. That way it measures the prompt itself. It covers five cases:

  1. The blueprint's sample order, with a conditional direction and a relative deadline.
  2. An order containing an embedded instruction that tries to make the AI approve tasks and invent a payment direction.
  3. An order with no dates at all.
  4. Garbled text.
  5. An order in Hindi.

Every case also checks two rules: relative dates are never turned into calendar dates, and no due date is proposed unless the order states one. Every prompt has a version number, and each analysis records which prompt and model produced it.

Honest demos

If the live AI is unavailable during judging, the app can replay a recorded real run of the sample order. The replay carries a visible label in the app. We will not present replayed output as live, and we will not claim a task was created if the backend failed.

Design decisions worth noting

  • One authorisation path. Because all writes go through functions, there is exactly one place that enforces permissions, field allow-lists and auditing. Security rules simply deny client writes.
  • Typed end to end. Every callable function's input and output is defined once as a Zod schema in the shared package. A contract change breaks the build on both sides instead of failing at runtime.
  • Reliable background jobs. Each job has a deterministic ID, a claim with a time-limited lease, and a run counter. A retried or duplicated job cannot process a document twice, and a manual re-run gets a fresh job.
  • Large text stays out of the database. Extracted page text and search chunks are stored as private files next to the original, keeping Firestore documents small and fast.
  • Swappable AI. The model sits behind an AiProvider interface. A fixture provider replays recorded runs for tests, local development and the labelled demo fallback; the live provider calls Claude.
  • Data residency. Firestore, Storage and Functions all run in asia-south1. This region is fixed at creation, so we set it on day zero.

Security and privacy

Legal files carry privileged communications, personal data and financial records, so we treated confidentiality as a product requirement from the first commit, not as a final polish step.

Isolation between organisations

Every case, document, task and log belongs to exactly one organisation. Firestore security rules allow a user to read an organisation's data only while they are an active member of it. The server checks membership again on every call, because server code bypasses the rules. Automated tests confirm that one organisation cannot read another's cases, and that cross-case queries for the task list and calendar return only permitted records.

Access control

  • Roles are enforced on the server for every action, using the same permission matrix the app uses to hide controls.
  • Invitations can only be accepted by a signed-in user whose verified email matches the invitation.
  • An upload must match a reservation created by the server for that case. A user cannot write files into another case's folder or overwrite an existing file.
  • The Anthropic API key lives in Secret Manager and is used only by server functions. It never reaches the browser.

Untrusted files

Uploaded documents are treated as hostile until checked. The server verifies the real file signature, rejects encrypted PDFs and caps decompressed Word file size to stop archive bombs. Files that fail are deleted, and the user sees a plain-language reason.

Confidential data stays out of side channels

  • Logs and audit entries carry IDs, field names and status values only, never document text, quotes or case titles.
  • The service worker caches only the app shell. Case data is cached for offline reading only on devices the user marks as trusted, and signing out wipes that cache.
  • Documents are read through authenticated SDK calls, never through permanent public download links.

Auditability

Every significant action writes an audit entry in the same transaction as the change: uploads, analyses, each review decision, task and event changes, and team changes. If the change happens, its record exists; if the transaction fails, neither does. Owners and admins can read the full log, and each case shows its own activity feed.

Compliance in India

All data is stored in the Mumbai region. Before any real client data is used, a production launch would need a review of professional confidentiality duties, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and its rules, and the AI provider's data-use and retention terms. Until then, CasePilot runs only on fictional documents, and we make no claim that it is production-certified.

Impact and how we measure it

CasePilot's value is time and certainty: the hours spent reading orders and copying directions into diaries become minutes of review, and every date in the system can be traced to its source.

Expected impact

  • For a solo advocate: each new order produces a checked to-do list and calendar entries without retyping. The phone app shows what is due today, even between courtrooms.
  • For a law firm: partners can see who owns each direction, which dates are still unconfirmed and what is overdue, across every matter, without asking on WhatsApp.
  • For a corporate legal team: a single, auditable record of obligations across disputes and regulatory matters, ready for internal reporting and handovers to external counsel.
  • For clients: fewer missed steps, and a lawyer who can answer "what happens next?" with a source.

Closing statement

Lawyers do not just need a place to store case files. They need to know what each document means for their next action, and they need to be able to prove where that answer came from.

CasePilot AI reads a court order, extracts its directions and dates, links every finding to its page, and proposes an action plan. The lawyer reviews, approves and assigns the work, and the team tracks it to completion in one shared workspace, with every step on record.

Our vision is to help Indian legal professionals spend less time organising case information and more time on the work that needs their expertise. From legal documents to actionable case workflows, with the lawyer always in control.
